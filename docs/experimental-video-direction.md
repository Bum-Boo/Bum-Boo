# Experimental video direction

This note defines how the Live Lab translates cultural and technical references into original moving material studies. It is a constraint document, not a prompt library for copying an existing object.

## Reference translation

### Sukajan without product imitation

Yokosuka City's official guide describes sukajan as lustrous jackets embroidered with motifs such as tigers, dragons, and eagles, and connects their postwar souvenir history to Dobuita Street.[1] The Live Lab borrows only the material grammar: aged rayon-like sheen, dense thread direction, rib knit, zipper, puckering, frayed seams, and an asymmetric cropped motif. It does not reproduce a shop's design, brand mark, lettering, flag, or complete jacket.

A Meiji-period sashiko jacket in The Met collection is used as a separate reference for visible repair, stitch density, practical reinforcement, and textile aging.[4]

### Tiger as material tension

The tiger references are treated as studies of brush rhythm and folk-art freedom rather than a photoreal character. The National Museum of Korea's tiger collection feature and The Met's ink tiger are used to observe asymmetry, flattened anatomy, dry brush, and the relation between animal form and negative space.[2][3]

The deployed motion study removes the face and complete body. Only torn paper, five dry-brush stripes, a flat stitched paw fragment, and loose thread remain. This prevents the video model from turning a cultural motif into a living mascot or fantasy animal.

### Electricity as a physical mechanism

The Science Museum Group's moving-coil relay provides the mechanical vocabulary: exposed coil, armature, spring contact, oxidized metal, screws, solder, and insulated wire.[5] The generated shot therefore depicts a millimetre-scale contact movement rather than neon current, HUD graphics, arbitrary circuit text, or a futuristic dashboard.

### Language as paper structure

The language/access study uses washi and hanji as material references for fibre, deckled edges, binding, repair tape, rubbing, and blind pressure marks.[6] It deliberately contains no readable pseudo-Hangul or decorative calligraphy. Because the generated motion introduced an overlarge page transformation, this project uses the approved still rather than forcing a weak loop into the site.

## Anti-synthetic motion contract

Each deployed clip follows the same rules:

1. Start from an approved physical still rather than asking a text model to invent a full scene.
2. Keep a locked or near-locked camera and one continuous shot.
3. Allow one small material event: a relay contact, a hinged gate, a satin ripple, a paper edge, or a moving reflection.
4. Keep object count, silhouette, joins, stitch routes, and layer boundaries stable.
5. Exclude people, faces, readable text, logos, brands, watermarks, UI, neon current, and cinematic scene changes.
6. Reject a result when a flat animal becomes alive, a page appears from nowhere, or geometry is regularized over time.
7. Inspect the complete timeline, not only a hero frame.
8. Build the web loop from stable frame boundaries, remove duplicate turnaround frames, and retain a static poster.
9. Load only the selected project's asset. With reduced motion enabled, omit video from the DOM and render its poster.
